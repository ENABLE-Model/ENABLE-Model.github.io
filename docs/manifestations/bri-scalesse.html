Bri Scalesse

When women with disabilities navigate the fashion and media industries, the modelling agency and brands use Bri Scalesse to foreground disability representation and accessible inclusion.

ENABLE Model location​

  • Navigator-side Compensations → Use Assistive Technologies

What it is​

Bri Scalesse works within the fashion, media, and disability-advocacy system to model, write, and speak publicly about access, representation, and the lived experience of wheelchair users inside industries that often exclude them. She uses her platform as a wheelchair-user, model, and writer to challenge ableist norms in modelling and brand representation. (FASHION Magazine) Because the systemic accessibility of fashion/media often fails, Bri’s activity constitutes a post‐launch compensation: she navigates and compensates for the neglect of inclusion -- rather than participating only in early design of the industry. The burden shifts onto her (and by extension other disabled people) to make visible what the industry omits.

Why it matters​

Within the fashion-media system, default representations exclude disability, and the labour of inclusion often lands on disabled people themselves. Bri’s work matters because it reveals the exclusionary norms (e.g., campaigns without wheelchair-users, inaccessible runways) and acts to remediate them -- through modelling, public commentary, and advocacy. Her role highlights how access does not just require assistive technologies but also cultural re-calibration of what bodies count. Because she takes on both modelling and advocacy, she simultaneously occupies the space of visibility and of compensatory work for inaccessible environments. This redistribution of labour -- from industry builders to disabled individuals -- realigns who must act to make access real.

Real-world example​

Bri began modelling after an auto accident at age six left her legs paralysed and she became a wheelchair user. (FASHION Magazine) She has modelled for global brands such as Nike, UGG, Skims, Tommy Hilfiger, and others. (We Speak Model Management) In interviews she describes how early modelling jobs placed her in inaccessible dressing rooms (down a flight of stairs) and segregated away from other models, forcing her to advocate for her own inclusion. (FASHION Magazine) Her social-media presence (TikTok, Instagram) amplifies issues like wheelchair damage during air travel (her chair named “Aphrodite” was broken during a flight) and the emotional labour of reclaiming her body in modelling spaces. (FASHION Magazine)

What care sounds like​

“When I’m on the runway, I feel really powerful. I feel present in my body.” (FASHION Magazine)

“I don’t think it should be on the model to have to fight so hard to exist somewhere in the same space as other models.” (FASHION Magazine)

“My chair is my freedom.” Meet Model Bri Scalesse: ‘My Chair Is My Freedom’

These quotes register what care might sound like: the environment enables her full presence, her body’s integrity remains intact, and she feels included rather than separated.

What neglect sounds like​

“The models’ designated dressing room was located down a flight of stairs, with no way for her to descend safely.” (FASHION Magazine)

“I felt like I just didn’t have a place, quite literally.” (FASHION Magazine)

These examples show neglect: inaccessible spaces, segregation of disabled people, and the absence of structural inclusion.

What compensation sounds like​

“Having to speak up for myself. But I shouldn’t have to.” (FASHION Magazine)

She recounts “took about six weeks for her to get used to her new custom chair” after her old chair broke during air travel. (FASHION Magazine)

These reflect the compensation labour she undertakes: advocating for access, repairing personal equipment when infrastructure fails, and absorbing the emotional/physical cost of adaptation. The burden shifts onto her rather than being designed out by the system.

All observations occur within the context of the fashion and media production system (modelling, brand campaigns, social-media representation).

📝 Disclaimer

The ENABLE Model draws on principles from anthropology and journalism to create a public ethnography of accessibility, documenting how people intervene or compensate for accessibility breakdowns in the real world. Inclusion here does not imply endorsement. It chronicles observed use -- how a tool, organization, or strategy is actually used -- rather than how it is marketed. References, when provided, are for verification and transparency.
