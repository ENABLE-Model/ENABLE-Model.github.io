Howe Innovation Center

Disability-tech founders work with the Howe Innovation Center to connect their products with disabled users for testing and feedback before launch -- shifting user research and QA testing upstream.

ENABLE Model location

  • Builder-side Interventions → Set Requirements that Include Accessibility
  • Builder-side Interventions → Design Accessible Experiences
  • Builder-side Interventions → Test for Accessibility
  • Builder-side Interventions → Iterate to Address Shortcomings

What it is​

The Howe Innovation Center, launched in May 2023, is part of Perkins School for the Blind. Named after Perkins' founding director Samuel Gridley Howe, the center connects entrepreneurs and technology companies with disabled people to co-design accessible solutions for employment, education, and daily living.1

Perkins' access to children and adults with a range of disabilities positions the center to provide what most startups lack: direct contact with the people they claim to serve. The center offers market research, user testing, and feedback loops that let companies work with their target users during development, not after launch.2

The center maintains a database of 750+ assistive technology companies globally -- 40% U.S.-based, with the UK, Israel, India, and Australia rounding out the top five markets.3 Partnerships with MIT, Olin College of Engineering, and McKinsey & Company provide additional research capacity.1

Why it matters​

The Howe Innovation Center addresses a structural gap in how disability technology gets built: most startups develop products about disabled people without meaningful input from disabled people until late in the process -- if at all.

The upstream intervention: By connecting founders with disabled users at the requirement-setting and design stages, Howe shifts user feedback upstream. This contrasts with the typical pattern where disabled users encounter finished products and must compensate for design decisions made without them.

The structural advantage: Perkins' existing community provides what individual startups cannot easily build: trust relationships with disabled users who are willing to test early-stage products. Without such access, founders often substitute assumptions for research -- a pattern that produces disability dongles and products that require extensive user workarounds.

The remaining gap: The center cannot guarantee that startups will act on user feedback. Connection to disabled users is necessary but not sufficient for accessible outcomes. The ENABLE Model would ask: do the products that emerge from Howe programs actually reduce navigator-side burden, or do they become disability dongles -- solutions that look good to funders but fail the people they claim to serve?

Real-world examples​

In the news

Perkins' New Howe Innovation Center Designed to Accelerate Accessibility (May 2023)
-- Watertown News

  • The center launched with the explicit goal of connecting innovators with disabled users during development. Executive Director Sandy Lacey: "By amplifying the lived experience of people with disabilities and creating programs that support innovation, we eliminate friction points for entrepreneurs and accelerate product innovation."

Howe Innovation Center at Perkins Hosts DisabilityTech Pitch Challenge With Mitsubishi Electric (October 2025)
-- Business Wire

  • Nine startups showcased assistive technologies at Howe's pitch challenge. Tatum Robotics won the $5,000 grand prize for a robotic system that translates English into tactile fingerspelling for DeafBlind users -- an example of builder-side development informed by specific user needs.

Perkins and Adaptation Ventures Advance a New Path for DisabilityTech Investment (2025)
-- PR Newswire

  • Howe partnered with Adaptation Ventures, a pre-seed VC fund focused on disability tech, to address the "first money in" funding gap. Lacey: "Investment in DisabilityTech companies is growing, but access to 'first money in' remains a barrier for founders." Early-stage disability tech investment grew from $818 million (2022) to $1.1 billion (2024), yet founders still identify early funding as their biggest obstacle.4

Mapping the Disability Tech Market with Perkins School for the Blind
-- Equal Entry

  • Howe's market research found 140+ U.S. companies in low-vision navigation alone -- with 23 glasses-based startups raising $135 million collectively. Keith Kirkland of Wear.Works advised: "Don't build a blind device. Build a device everybody can use but optimize for the blind experience."3
  • Olin College's Paul Ruvolo noted the partnership helps engineering students "design, build, and engineer a more inclusive world" -- integrating disability perspectives into engineering education rather than treating accessibility as an afterthought.1

What care sounds like (builder-side interventions)​

Care at the requirement-setting and testing stages involves building with disabled users, not for them:

  • "By amplifying the lived experience of people with disabilities and creating programs that support innovation, we eliminate friction points for entrepreneurs."1 -- Sandy Lacey, Executive Director
  • "Don't build a blind device. Build a device everybody can use but optimize for the blind experience."3 -- Keith Kirkland, Wear.Works
  • "[We] connect real users to real entrepreneurs and technologists, and together, we can solve real accessibility problems."2

What neglect sounds like (builder-side interventions)​

Neglect involves building disability technology without disabled input -- skipping requirement-setting and testing with actual users:

  • "We'll do user testing once we have a working prototype."
  • "We interviewed caregivers and clinicians -- they understand the needs."
  • "The technology works; users will adapt to it."
  • "We don't have access to disabled users at this stage."

What compensation sounds like (navigator-side compensations)​

Compensation describes what happens when disability tech is built without user input -- users must work around design failures or give feedback that may be ignored:

  • "I was excited about this app until I tried to use it with my screen reader."
  • "The device solves a problem I don't have while ignoring the one I do." -- a disability dongle
  • "Another product designed by people who've never met anyone like me."
  • "I gave feedback during beta testing. They shipped it anyway without changes."

All observations occur within the context of disability technology development and venture funding in the United States.

📝 Disclaimer

The ENABLE Model draws on the principles of anthropology and the practice of journalism to create a public ethnography of accessibility, documenting how people intervene or compensate for accessibility breakdowns in the real world. Inclusion here does not imply endorsement. It chronicles observed use -- how a tool, organization, or strategy is actually used -- rather than how it is marketed. References, when provided, are for verification and transparency.
