Google

Disabled people use Google's accessibility features -- Live Caption, TalkBack, ChromeVox, Lookout -- to navigate digital life, while also absorbing the labor of adapting when platform updates break workflows they depend on.

What it is​

Google develops and maintains browsers (Chrome), operating systems (Android, ChromeOS), productivity suites (Workspace), and developer platforms that structure daily work for billions of people. The company's Central Accessibility Team, led by Eve Andersson since 2013, builds features like Live Caption (real-time captions for any audio), TalkBack (Android screen reader), Lookout (object and text recognition for blind people), and Live Transcribe (speech-to-text for deaf people).12

At the same time, schools, governments, and employers mandate Google tools through institutional procurement, concentrating platform governance in ways that limit user exit. When Google iterates to improve accessibility, millions benefit. When updates introduce regressions or when Workspace products lag behind accessibility standards, disabled people who cannot opt out must compensate -- rebuilding workflows, switching browsers, or filing feedback that may or may not be addressed.

Why it matters​

Google illustrates how the same organization can simultaneously demonstrate builder-side care and create conditions of precarity for disabled people:

Care: Live Caption and TalkBack represent genuine accessibility design and development. Andersson's philosophy -- "the accessibility problems of today can lead to the technology breakthroughs of tomorrow" -- has produced features like voice typing and autocomplete that became mainstream after originating as accessibility tools.3 Google's 2024 expansion of Live Caption to seven additional languages and the introduction of Expressive Captions (conveying tone and environmental cues, not just words) demonstrate ongoing iteration.4

Precarity: When institutions mandate Google Workspace without exit options, disabled people absorb platform instability. Browser updates can break screen reader compatibility; Workspace products like Google Sites have documented accessibility gaps; the recommended browser/screen reader combinations differ across products, forcing people to maintain multiple configurations.56 This creates precarity -- disabled people depend on platforms they cannot control and must rebuild workflows when updates change behavior.

Abandonment risk: Screen reader users report fearing updates because "updates causing changes/severe bugs to screen reader" have occurred without warning.7 When accessibility features regress without acknowledgment or timeline for repair, this constitutes a form of abandonment -- access that once existed becomes unreliable.

The ENABLE Model traces this as a redistribution of labor: Google's scale means both its care and its neglect propagate across entire sectors. When accessibility works, it works at scale. When it fails, disabled people in schools and workplaces that mandated Google tools have no alternative but to compensate.

Real-world examples​

In the news

How Designing For Disabled People Is Giving Google An Edge (2016, still cited)
-- Fast Company

  • Eve Andersson's philosophy -- "the accessibility problems of today can lead to the technology breakthroughs of tomorrow" -- helped produce features like voice typing and autocomplete that became mainstream after originating as accessibility tools. This builder-side approach treats accessibility as innovation, not compliance.

Resolved issue with Google Chrome, NVDA and math (December 2024)
-- Perkins School for the Blind

  • Google Chrome stopped rendering math correctly for NVDA users. Perkins advised users to switch to Firefox as a workaround, noting "Google's accessibility team has been notified of the issue, but no timeline was provided for a fix." When platform updates break accessibility, users may need to compensate by switching to alternatives while waiting for fixes.

Google Update - Vision Related Accessibility Enhancements from Q3 2024 (October 2024)
-- American Foundation for the Blind AccessWorld

  • Google's Q3 2024 updates added AI-powered OCR to ChromeOS Select-to-Speak, "enabling text-to-speech for inaccessible or scanned PDFs directly in the Chrome PDF viewer." The AFB also documented five new help center articles for Workspace screen reader users -- showing active iteration and accessibility documentation maintenance.
  • University IT departments document that "due to the accessibility issues that exist with Google Sites, it is recommended that web developers who utilize a screen reader use other web publishing services." The University of Colorado's accessibility guide notes that for Google Drive, "the combination of JAWS for Windows and Firefox may be used but may not provide full support within the Google Drive interface."5

What care sounds like (builder-side interventions)​

Care at Google involves treating accessibility as a requirement, not a feature request:

  • "Accessibility is a basic human right. It benefits everyone." -- Eve Andersson, Senior Director of Products for All3
  • "Block the release until the screen reader flow works end to end."
  • "Treat accessibility failures as production bugs, not enhancements."
  • "We're expanding Live Caption to seven more languages so deaf people worldwide can participate."

What neglect sounds like (builder-side interventions)​

Neglect involves shipping updates without accessibility verification or treating disabled people as edge cases:

  • "Ship now and fix accessibility later."
  • "That regression affects too few users to block the launch."
  • "We recommend users switch to Firefox as a workaround." (rather than fixing Chrome)
  • "Due to the accessibility issues that exist with Google Sites, it is recommended that web developers who utilize a screen reader use other web publishing services."5

What compensation sounds like (navigator-side compensations)​

Compensation describes the labor disabled people undertake when Google's accessibility iteration lags or regresses:

  • "I'm afraid of updating my Chromebook due to updates causing changes/severe bugs to screen reader."7
  • "I spin up a Windows virtual machine just to finish assignments everyone else completes natively."
  • "I relearn the interface after every update because shortcuts and focus order keep changing."
  • "For now, consider using the Firefox web browser as an alternative" -- the workaround blind users must adopt when Chrome breaks.8
  • "To make a doc accessible for most people, it is best to convert a Google Doc to a Microsoft Word document."5

All observations occur within the context of the global commercial technology platform governance system, where institutional mandates concentrate user dependence on platforms whose accessibility varies across products and updates.

📝 Disclaimer

The ENABLE Model draws on the principles of anthropology and the practice of journalism to create a public ethnography of accessibility, documenting how people intervene or compensate for accessibility breakdowns in the real world. Inclusion here does not imply endorsement. It chronicles observed use -- how a tool, organization, or strategy is actually used -- rather than how it is marketed. References, when provided, are for verification and transparency.
