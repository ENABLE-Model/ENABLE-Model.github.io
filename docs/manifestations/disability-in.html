Disability:IN

Disabled job seekers and employees consult the Disability Index to identify companies that have benchmarked their disability inclusion practices -- a voluntary, self-reported score that companies earn by answering their own questions.

ENABLE Model location

  • Builder-side Interventions → Set Requirements that Include Accessibility

What it is​

Disability:IN is a nonprofit organization that works with corporations to advance disability inclusion in the workplace. Founded as the US Business Leadership Network, it co-created the Disability Equality Index in 2015 with the American Association of People with Disabilities (AAPD). The index is a voluntary annual benchmarking survey that rates corporations on disability inclusion practices. Companies complete approximately 76 yes-or-no questions across categories including culture and leadership, enterprise-wide access, employment practices, community engagement, and supplier diversity. Companies that score 80 or above out of 100 receive the designation "Best Place to Work for Disability Inclusion."1

The index grew from 80 participating companies at launch to 542 US companies by 2024, including over 70% of the Fortune 100.2 In 2024, Disability:IN internationalized the index, expanding to Brazil, Canada, Germany, India, Japan, the Philippines, and the United Kingdom.3 By 2025 the index counted 655 participating companies across eight countries.4

In early February 2025, within days of the Trump administration's executive orders targeting DEI programs signed January 20, the index's name changed. Registration forms, URLs, and the Disability:IN website shifted from "Disability Equality Index" to "Disability Index." The old logo, which included the word "Equality," was replaced with one that did not. No public announcement accompanied the change. AAPD, the co-owner of the index, continues to use the name "Disability Equality Index" on its own website. Neither organization has explained the discrepancy.56

The ENABLE Model observes Disability:IN at builder-side requirement-setting: the point where obligations around accessibility and inclusion are established. The index attempts to create requirements for corporate disability inclusion by benchmarking and publicly recognizing companies that meet its standards.

Why it matters​

Before the Disability Equality Index, there was no standardized tool for measuring corporate disability inclusion. Companies made unverified claims about disability inclusion in job postings and recruiting materials. Disabled job seekers had no basis for comparing employer practices. Accommodation rates, ERG existence, leadership representation by disability status, etc, were not tracked or disclosed in any standardized form. The index intervened on that default condition: it created a common vocabulary, a set of questions corporations had to engage with annually, and a public designation that gave disabled job seekers a signal - however imperfect - about which employers had made a measurable commitment.

The index did not create the corporate obligation to address disability employment. Section 503 of the Rehabilitation Act, enacted in 1973, has required federal contractors to take affirmative action to employ people with disabilities since before most of the companies now participating in the index were founded.7 A 7% disability utilization goal, added to Section 503 in 2013, gave that requirement a measurable target against which compliance could be reviewed.7 The voluntary Disability Equality Index arrived forty years into that mandatory framework, offering a designation to companies not covered by Section 503 and a reputational signal to those that were. The network that created it was not a spontaneous corporate initiative. The U.S. Business Leadership Network -- Disability:IN's predecessor -- was created in 1994 by the President's Committee on Employment of People with Disabilities, a federal body whose own roots trace to Truman's 1947 effort to organize post-war employment for veterans with disabilities.89 Corporate disability inclusion as a formal practice was built by government and later sustained by market incentives. The index created a voluntary benchmark alongside a mandatory framework that preceded it by decades.

655 companies participated in the 2025 Disability Index. Participation is voluntary. Scores are self-reported. No external body verifies the responses.

The index's methodology asks what companies say they do, not what they do. Companies complete the survey themselves, without audits, site visits, or employee testimony. A company can describe a disability inclusion program in its survey response and receive credit regardless of whether disabled employees experience it. Disability advocates have named this pattern inclusion washing -- elsewhere called purple washing.1011 Organizational sociologists call the underlying dynamic decoupling: organizations adopt policies symbolically without implementing them substantively. The structure of the index makes decoupling easy. When the index asks whether companies offer flexible work, 99% report they do -- but the index does not ask what that means in practice. Dell received a perfect score while its return-to-office policy precludes remote workers from receiving promotions, which disability advocates note directly harms employees who need remote work to participate in the labor force.10 Companies including Amazon, Google, and Goldman Sachs have received top scores while facing active disability discrimination lawsuits and settlements in the same years they were designated Best Places to Work for Disability Inclusion.12 The index does not reconcile these records. The designation stands. Across all participating companies, just 4% of employees identify as disabled -- a figure that has barely changed since 2019, when it was 3.7%, even as participation has grown from 80 to 655 companies.10 One in four U.S. workers has a disability or health condition.10 That gap is not measurement error. It reflects disclosure decisions shaped by stigma, fear of discrimination, and employees' rational calculation about whether identifying as disabled is safe at work. An index that measures what companies say they do cannot close a gap driven by what disabled employees do not say.

The ENABLE Model does not resolve whether a more rigorous version of the index would serve disabled people better. A verified, audited benchmark would expose companies to reputational risk when they fail, cost more to participate in, and require Disability:IN to fund verification mechanisms it does not currently have. Participation might drop from 655 companies to a fraction of that. The index doesn't answer whether disabled job seekers are better served by a rigorous benchmark that most large employers decline or a permissive one that most accept. If corporations will only participate in disability inclusion benchmarking when it is unverified and comfortable, then it also doesn't answer the question of what disabled employees can actually rely on. The problem may be deeper than verification. Legal scholars studying workplace diversity standards have argued that organizations can comply authentically with a standard that, by its construction, cannot deliver the equality it names -- because the standard was built to demonstrate employer good faith rather than to produce equal outcomes.13 If that argument holds, auditing the answers does not change what the answers produce.

The rebranding has not been explained. The change from "Disability Equality Index" to "Disability Index" removed the word that shared an acronym with Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion at the precise moment that acronym became politically costly. The timing is documented. Disability:IN's reasoning is not. The organization issued no press release, no announcement, and no statement connecting the name change to the political environment. An independent blogger discovered the change by noticing that URLs had quietly redirected, registration forms had changed, and the Disability:IN website sitemap no longer contained the word "equality."5 The ADA names equality of opportunity as one of its four central goals, alongside full participation, independent living, and economic self-sufficiency.14 "Equality" is in the foundational language of disability rights law. Its removal from the name of the field's most widely used corporate disability inclusion benchmark was not announced, not explained, and not discussed. The question the ENABLE Model asks is what it means that the explanation was withheld. A disabled job seeker who knew the Disability Equality Index now encounters the Disability Index. The navigator must determine whether it is the same tool. The rebrand imposed a navigator-side labor.

The ENABLE Model does not resolve whether the name change was the right call. The alternative to removing a word may have been dissolution of the index at the moment it was most needed. If quietly dropping "equality" from the name preserved a benchmark that 655 companies and millions of disabled employees rely on, then survival under duress, even silent survival, may have been the least bad option available. What the ENABLE Model names is the silence around it. The disabled job seekers who navigated using this benchmark may have benefitted from this information.

The association with the corporate DEI framework created real avenues for disabled employment. Disability inclusion rode DEI momentum into corporate structures where it might not otherwise have reached. But that housing also meant disability inclusion never had to build independent institutional footing inside those corporations. When the Trump administration issued executive orders targeting DEI programs in January 2025, major corporations including Meta, Amazon, Target, and Goldman Sachs publicly rolled back DEI commitments within weeks.15 Disability inclusion retreated with them, rather than standing on infrastructure it had built separately. The rebranding makes that dependency visible. DEI participation carried reputational and commercial benefit to companies. Disability:IN's own materials cited research showing companies excelling at disability inclusion realized 1.6x revenue and 2.6x net income compared to peers.16 The index had no enforcement mechanism independent of those incentives. Corporate participation grew to 655 companies in 2025, up from 542 in 2024. Whether the commitments behind those scores grew with the participation numbers is not documented.

In the same political moment, mandatory enforcement contracted. The Trump administration proposed eliminating the 7% disability utilization goal under Section 503 and moved to defund OFCCP, the agency responsible for auditing contractor compliance with disability affirmative action requirements.17 The 7% goal was not a quota. It was an aspirational benchmark with enforcement review attached -- the closest the federal system had to a measurable, verified standard for corporate disability employment. As that mechanism weakened, voluntary index participation grew. Whether voluntary benchmarking expands to fill the space that mandatory enforcement vacates, or grows precisely because it costs nothing to join and requires nothing to remain, is the political economy question the participation figures do not answer.

The precarity this reveals is not unique to Disability:IN, and the political rollback is not without precedent. In the early 1980s, the Reagan administration attempted to weaken Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. Disability rights advocates led by Patrisha Wright and Evan Kemp Jr. organized a campaign of more than 40,000 cards and letters, and the administration dropped the rollback in 1984.18 That resistance succeeded partly because the target was specific: a named regulation, a named agency, a named action. The 2025 rollback arrives differently -- distributed across executive orders targeting DEI broadly, administrative rulemaking on Section 503 specifically, and corporate calculations about reputational cost. The voluntary index survives that distribution in a way that mandatory enforcement cannot, because it has no enforcement mechanism to remove. The precarity page documents how disability inclusion collapses when it depends on political conditions that can be withdrawn. A voluntary benchmark with no enforcement mechanism and self-reported scoring is the kind of structure that works when institutional conditions favor it and goes quiet when they do not. The disabled employees and job seekers who used the Disability Equality Index to identify genuinely inclusive employers are now navigating a market where the index survives, the designation continues, and the signal it sends has been complicated by the circumstances of its own survival.

Disability:IN did not create the structural conditions this page observes. It did not make DEI the institutional housing disability inclusion had to live in to reach corporate structures at scale. It did not design a certification market in which verified benchmarking may have been economically untenable. It did not author the political moment that may have made "equality" a liability in the name of a disability inclusion index. It did not build the voluntary compliance incentive structure that makes decoupling rational for corporate participants. What the ENABLE Model observes is what those conditions produced when they converged on an organization doing genuine work in an architecture it inherited.

Real-world examples​

In the news

The 10th Annual Disability Equality Index Shows Disability Inclusion Progress, But Reporting Gaps Remain (2024)
-- Kristen Parisi, HR Brew

  • HR Brew reported on the 2024 Disability Equality Index, noting that while participation grew, self-reporting gaps raised questions about whether scores reflected lived employee experience. Disability advocate Kole Petersen described the pattern as "inclusion washing" and called for employee testimonies alongside corporate surveys. The article documents the tension at the core of builder-side/requirement-setting: when the requirement is voluntary and unverified, what is being required?
In the news

The Disability Equality Index: A Measure of Trust Causing Distrust (2024)
-- Kole Petersen, The Catalyst News

  • The Catalyst News documented that companies including Amazon, Google, and Goldman Sachs received top scores on the Disability Equality Index in years when they also faced active disability discrimination lawsuits and settlements. The article asks what a perfect score means when the companies holding it are simultaneously defendants in disability discrimination proceedings. The index does not reconcile these records.
In the news

Is Disability:IN quietly renaming the Disability Equality Index (DEI)? (2025)
-- Ashlee, Semantic Fish Net

  • An independent blogger documented the transition from "Disability Equality Index" to "Disability Index" through URL redirects, changed registration forms, a new logo without the word "Equality," and the absence of any public statement from Disability:IN. The post was the first published documentation of the name change. Disability:IN did not respond to inquiry emails. AAPD continued to use the old name on its own website without explanation.
  • The Trump administration signed executive orders targeting DEI programs on January 20, 2025. Within weeks, Meta, Amazon, Target, Goldman Sachs, and Deloitte publicly rolled back DEI commitments. The Disability Equality Index became the Disability Index in the same window.15
  • The Regulatory Review documented that the administration moved to eliminate the 7% disability employment utilization goal for federal contractors, explicitly connecting it to the anti-DEI executive order, in September 2025.19
  • AAPD continues to use the name "Disability Equality Index" on its own website as of this writing. Neither AAPD nor Disability:IN has issued a public statement on the name discrepancy.6
  • Corporate participation in the index grew from 542 companies in 2024 to 655 in 2025, after the rebranding.4

What care sounds like (builder-side interventions)​

Care at the requirement-setting stage involves using a disability inclusion benchmark to drive genuine change, not to earn recognition:

  • "We use the index to find gaps, not to earn a designation."
  • "We scored 70 this year. Here is what we are fixing before we submit next year."
  • "Our disability employee resource group reviewed our responses before we submitted."
  • "We participated and then audited whether our answers matched our employees' experience."
  • "We're proud of the designation. Here is the accommodation data behind it."

What neglect sounds like (builder-side interventions)​

Neglect at the requirement-setting stage involves treating the score as an endpoint, disconnecting the survey from the people who know the answers, or using the designation as a shield:

  • "We scored 100, so we're set."
  • "Our PR team handled the survey responses."
  • "A complaint came in but we're on the Best Places to Work list."
  • "We submitted without asking our disability ERG to review the answers."
  • "The score covers us."

What compensation sounds like (navigator-side compensations)​

Compensation describes the labor disabled job seekers and employees carry when the tools meant to identify inclusive employers do not verify what they claim to measure:

  • "I looked up the Disability Index score before accepting my offer. I had no way to know if it meant anything."
  • "My company got a top score the same year they denied my accommodation request."
  • "I used the list to narrow my job search. I don't know if I should still trust it."
  • "Nobody told me the name changed. I'm not sure if it's the same thing."
  • "I found out about the rebranding from a blog post, not from the organization."

All observations occur within the context of voluntary corporate disability inclusion benchmarking in the United States, where the most widely used benchmark in the field renamed itself without public explanation within days of federal executive orders targeting DEI programs, and where the disabled employees and job seekers who used that benchmark to navigate the labor market were not told.

📝 Disclaimer

The ENABLE Model draws on the principles of anthropology and the practice of journalism to create a public ethnography of accessibility, documenting how people intervene or compensate for accessibility breakdowns in the real world. Inclusion here does not imply endorsement. It chronicles observed use -- how a tool, organization, or strategy is actually used -- rather than how it is marketed. References, when provided, are for verification and transparency.
